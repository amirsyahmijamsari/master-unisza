# CHAPTER 6: CONCLUSION

## 6.1 Introduction

This chapter presents the conclusions derived from the flood frequency analysis using L-moments conducted on rainfall data from twenty stations in Terengganu, Malaysia. The chapter summarizes the key findings in relation to each research objective, discusses the contributions of this study to the field, acknowledges the limitations encountered, provides recommendations for practitioners, and suggests directions for future research.

---

## 6.2 Summary of Findings

This research applied L-moments methodology to analyze rainfall frequency patterns in Terengganu, comparing Annual Maximum Series (AMS) and daily rainfall data approaches. The study successfully addressed all four research objectives:

### 6.2.1 Objective 1: Parameter Estimation Using L-Moments

The L-moments method was successfully applied to estimate distribution parameters for all twenty rainfall stations. Key findings include:

- **Annual Maximum Series**: L₁ (mean) ranged from 194.89 mm to 289.65 mm, with L-skewness (τ₃) values ranging from 0.024 to 0.467.

- **Daily Rainfall Series**: L₁ ranged from 18.76 mm to 23.43 mm, with consistently higher L-skewness values (τ₃ ≈ 0.47 to 0.55) indicating more right-skewed distributions.

- **Comparative Analysis**: Daily rainfall data exhibited more consistent L-moment patterns across stations, clustering tightly in the L-moment ratio diagram, while annual maxima showed greater variability.

The L-moments method demonstrated its robustness and suitability for parameter estimation in both annual and daily rainfall datasets, confirming the findings of Hosking (1990) and Hosking and Wallis (1997) regarding the advantages of L-moments for hydrological applications.

### 6.2.2 Objective 2: Best-Fitting Distribution Selection

Distribution selection based on the Mean Absolute Deviation Index (MADI) revealed important patterns:

- **Annual Maximum Series**: Multiple distributions provided best fits across stations, including 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) (40%), Generalized Logistic (20%), Pearson Type III (15%), Generalized Normal (10%), Generalized Pareto (10%), and Generalized Extreme Value (5%).

- **Daily Rainfall Series**: The 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) distribution was unanimously selected as the best-fitting distribution for all twenty stations (100%).

- **Overall Finding**: The 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) distribution (Hosking, 1994) emerged as the most suitable distribution for modeling rainfall in tropical Terengganu, consistent with findings from other tropical studies (Parida, 1999; Murshed et al., 2014).

The superior performance of the 4-Parameter Kappa distribution for daily data suggests that the additional flexibility provided by the fourth parameter is essential for capturing the complex rainfall patterns characteristic of tropical climates.

### 6.2.3 Objective 3: Return Period Analysis

Return values were successfully calculated for return periods ranging from 2 to 100 years:

- **100-year return values for Annual Maximum Series** ranged from 365.5 mm to 1124.9 mm across stations, representing a 3.1-fold variation.

- **100-year return values for Daily Rainfall Series** ranged from 129.9 mm to 169.3 mm, which are 2.8 to 6.6 times lower than their Annual Maximum counterparts.

- **Spatial variation**: Coastal stations exhibited higher return values, reflecting the influence of the Northeast Monsoon system. Station 0680071RF (Balai Polis Kg. Dura) showed the highest 100-year return value of 1124.9 mm for Annual Maximum Series.

- **Distribution impact**: The choice of distribution significantly affected extreme value estimates, particularly at longer return periods.

These findings provide valuable design rainfall estimates for infrastructure planning in Terengganu, though they highlight the importance of appropriate distribution selection for accurate estimation and demonstrate the substantial differences between Annual Maximum and Daily Rainfall approaches.

### 6.2.4 Objective 4: Overestimation Quantification

The comparison between Annual Maxima and daily data approaches revealed substantial overestimation at the 99th percentile, which represents extreme rainfall events critical for flood risk assessment:

- **Mean Overestimation Factor**: 8.50x at the 99th percentile

- **Range**: 7.14x to 9.99x across all 20 stations

- **Mean Overestimation Percentage**: 750.0%

- **Standard Deviation**: 0.91x, indicating consistent overestimation pattern across stations

This finding indicates that the Annual Maxima approach, when applied to tropical rainfall data, significantly underestimates the frequency of extreme events. Events that AM analysis suggests occur approximately once per year may actually occur approximately once every 43 days based on daily data analysis. This substantial overestimation has critical implications for flood risk assessment and infrastructure design in tropical regions, particularly for extreme events that are most relevant for flood management.

## 6.3 Conclusions

Based on the findings of this research, the following conclusions are drawn:

### 6.3.1 L-Moments Applicability

The L-moments method is highly applicable and effective for rainfall frequency analysis in tropical Malaysia. The method provides robust parameter estimates with lower variance compared to conventional moment methods, confirming its suitability for regions with limited hydrological records. The consistency of L-moment ratios across stations supports the potential for regional frequency analysis in future studies.

### 6.3.2 Distribution Selection

The 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) distribution is recommended as the primary distribution for rainfall frequency analysis in Terengganu. Its unanimous selection for daily data and frequent selection for annual maxima demonstrates its versatility in capturing the range of rainfall patterns observed in tropical climates. This finding aligns with the recommendations of Hosking (1994) regarding the use of flexible, multi-parameter distributions for complex hydrological data.

### 6.3.3 Limitations of Annual Maxima Approach

The Annual Maxima approach, while widely used and computationally convenient, significantly overestimates return periods when applied to tropical rainfall data. The mean overestimation factor of 8.50x at the 99th percentile indicates that extreme events may occur approximately 8.50 times more frequently than AM-based estimates suggest. This finding supports the concerns raised by Volpi et al. (2019) regarding data decimation in frequency analysis and demonstrates that the bias is particularly pronounced for extreme events, which are of greatest concern for flood risk management.

### 6.3.4 Importance of Daily Data Analysis

Analysis using daily rainfall data provides a more comprehensive representation of extreme event frequency in tropical climates. The higher sample sizes available from daily data (approximately 5,000 to 10,000 observations per station) enable more reliable distribution fitting and reduce estimation uncertainty compared to annual maxima (typically 30 to 50 observations).

### 6.3.5 Tropical Climate Considerations

Standard frequency analysis methodologies developed for temperate climates require careful adaptation when applied to tropical regions. The unique characteristics of tropical rainfall—including seasonal monsoon patterns, high intensity events, and multiple annual extremes—necessitate approaches that capture these features rather than assuming single annual peaks.

## 6.4 Contributions to Knowledge

This research makes the following contributions to the field of flood frequency analysis:

### 6.4.1 Theoretical Contribution

The study provides empirical evidence supporting the theoretical concerns about data decimation raised by Volpi et al. (2019). By quantifying the overestimation factor in a tropical setting, this research demonstrates that the concerns about traditional approaches are not merely theoretical but have practical implications for flood risk management.

### 6.4.2 Methodological Contribution

The comparative framework developed for assessing overestimation between AM and daily data approaches provides a replicable methodology that can be applied in other tropical regions. The use of L-moments ensures robust parameter estimation, while the MADI and MSDI criteria provide objective measures for distribution selection.

### 6.4.3 Regional Contribution

This research provides updated rainfall frequency estimates for Terengganu based on modern analytical methods. The L-moment statistics and distribution parameters presented can serve as reference values for future hydrological studies in the region.

### 6.4.4 Practical Contribution

The findings highlight the need for practitioners to consider daily data analysis in addition to traditional AM approaches when designing infrastructure in tropical regions. The documented overestimation factors provide a basis for adjusting design standards to account for the higher frequency of extreme events in tropical climates.

## 6.5 Limitations of the Study

Several limitations should be acknowledged:

### 6.5.1 Data Limitations

- **Temporal coverage**: Record lengths varied across stations (30 to 51 years for annual data), which may affect the reliability of extreme value estimates for some stations.

- **Spatial coverage**: The study focused on Terengganu state only. Results may not be directly applicable to other Malaysian states or tropical regions with different climatological characteristics.

- **Data quality**: While standard preprocessing was applied, potential issues with data homogeneity and undetected errors may affect some results.

### 6.5.2 Methodological Limitations

- **Independence assumption**: The analysis assumed independence between events, which may be violated during monsoon periods when extreme events cluster within short periods.

- **Stationarity assumption**: Climate change may affect the stationarity of rainfall patterns. This study did not explicitly address non-stationarity.

- **Threshold selection**: The 1 mm threshold for filtering daily data, while standard practice, may affect results. Sensitivity analysis with alternative thresholds was not conducted.

### 6.5.3 Analytical Limitations

- **Single-site analysis**: This study focused on at-site frequency analysis. Regional frequency analysis, which could improve estimation for data-sparse locations, was not conducted.

- **Uncertainty quantification**: Formal confidence intervals for return period estimates were not calculated. The reported values represent point estimates only.

- **Sub-daily analysis**: Only daily rainfall data were analyzed. Sub-daily (hourly) rainfall patterns, which are important for urban drainage design, were not considered.

## 6.6 Recommendations for Practice

Based on the findings of this research, the following recommendations are made for practitioners:

### 6.6.1 For Hydrologists and Engineers

1. **Dual Analysis Approach**: When designing flood mitigation infrastructure in tropical regions, conduct frequency analysis using both Annual Maxima and daily rainfall data. Compare the results and consider the more conservative estimates for critical infrastructure.

2. **Distribution Selection**: Use the 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) distribution as the primary candidate for rainfall frequency analysis in Malaysia. If computational limitations exist, the Generalized Logistic (GLO) or Generalized Extreme Value (GEV) distributions are suitable alternatives.

3. **Overestimation Adjustment**: Apply an adjustment factor to AM-based return period estimates when designing for tropical conditions. For extreme events (99th percentile), a factor of approximately 8.5x based on this study's findings should be considered for Terengganu. However, practitioners should conduct site-specific analysis as overestimation factors may vary by location and event magnitude.

### 6.6.2 For Policy Makers

1. **Design Standard Review**: Review existing design standards for flood infrastructure that are based solely on AM analysis. Consider updating guidelines to incorporate daily data analysis or adjustment factors for tropical conditions.

2. **Climate Adaptation**: Incorporate the findings regarding overestimation into climate change adaptation planning. If AM-based methods underestimate current flood frequency, the gap may widen under climate change scenarios.

3. **Investment Planning**: Recognize that infrastructure designed using AM-only methods may experience more frequent exceedances than anticipated. Plan for more regular maintenance and potential upgrades.

### 6.6.3 For Researchers

1. **L-Moments Adoption**: Adopt L-moments as the standard method for parameter estimation in frequency analysis, replacing conventional moment methods.

2. **Comprehensive Reporting**: Report MADI and MSDI values alongside distribution selection to enable comparison across studies.

3. **Data Sharing**: Maintain and share L-moment statistics to support regional frequency analysis efforts.

## 6.7 Suggestions for Future Research

The following directions for future research are recommended:

### 6.7.1 Regional Frequency Analysis

Extend this study to conduct regional frequency analysis across multiple states in Peninsular Malaysia. This would enable delineation of homogeneous regions, identification of regional distributions, and development of index-flood relationships for ungauged sites (Hosking & Wallis, 1997).

### 6.7.2 Non-Stationarity Assessment

Investigate the effects of climate change on rainfall frequency in Terengganu. Time-varying L-moments or other non-stationary frequency analysis methods could be applied to detect trends and project future changes (Khaliq et al., 2006; Salas & Obeysekera, 2014).

### 6.7.3 Sub-Daily Rainfall Analysis

Extend the analysis to sub-daily (hourly) rainfall data, which are critical for urban flood management and drainage design. The comparison between daily and sub-daily approaches would provide additional insights into appropriate methods for different applications.

### 6.7.4 Peaks-Over-Threshold Analysis

Compare the results of this study with Peaks-Over-Threshold (POT) or Partial Duration Series analysis. The POT approach may offer a middle ground between AM and daily data analysis while addressing the independence assumption more rigorously (Madsen et al., 1997).

### 6.7.5 Other Tropical Regions

Replicate this study in other tropical regions (Indonesia, Philippines, Thailand, tropical Africa, Amazon basin) to assess whether the overestimation patterns observed in Malaysia are generalizable. A global comparison would strengthen the evidence base for reforming frequency analysis practices in tropical climates.

### 6.7.6 Uncertainty Quantification

Develop and apply methods for quantifying uncertainty in overestimation factors. Bootstrap methods or Bayesian approaches could provide confidence intervals that better communicate the reliability of the findings.

### 6.7.7 Multivariate Analysis

Extend the analysis to multivariate frequency analysis, considering the joint distribution of rainfall intensity, duration, and frequency. This would provide more comprehensive information for infrastructure design.

### 6.7.8 Machine Learning Integration

Explore the integration of machine learning methods with L-moments for improved distribution selection and parameter estimation. Neural networks or ensemble methods might improve predictions, particularly for spatial interpolation.

## 6.8 Concluding Remarks

This research has demonstrated the applicability of L-moments for flood frequency analysis in tropical Malaysia and has quantified the substantial overestimation that occurs when traditional Annual Maxima methods are applied to tropical rainfall data. The key finding—that AM-based return periods overestimate by an average factor of 8.50x at the 99th percentile—has critical implications for flood risk management and infrastructure design in tropical regions, particularly for extreme events that trigger significant flooding.

The results support the call by Volpi et al. (2019) to "save hydrological observations" by using complete time-series analysis rather than decimating data to annual maxima. In tropical climates like Malaysia, where multiple extreme events occur within each year, the traditional AM approach discards valuable information that is essential for accurate risk assessment.

The 4-Parameter Kappa (K4D) distribution, with its four-parameter flexibility, has been identified as the most suitable distribution for modeling rainfall in Terengganu. This finding contributes to the growing body of evidence supporting the use of flexible distributions for complex hydrological data.

As climate change continues to alter precipitation patterns globally, the need for accurate frequency analysis methods becomes ever more critical. This research provides a foundation for improving flood frequency analysis practices in tropical regions, ultimately contributing to better-designed infrastructure and more resilient communities.

The journey toward improved flood risk management in tropical regions requires continued collaboration between researchers, practitioners, and policy makers. It is hoped that this research will contribute to that journey by highlighting the limitations of traditional methods and demonstrating the value of comprehensive analysis approaches that make full use of available hydrological observations.
